Molecular property prediction competition

We provide 308,402 molecules and their properties, calculated using quantum chemistry. You train a model to predict those properties from the atoms and bonds in a molecule. The goal is to reach the required accuracy as quickly as possible on one NVIDIA L40S GPU.

The predictions describe higher-energy states of a molecule’s electrons: how much energy it takes to reach them, and how strongly the molecule absorbs light in its first singlet transition. You do not need to run quantum-chemistry calculations; the training data already contain the answers.

How the competition works

  1. Train on the supplied molecules. Use the reference SMILES transformer, a graph network, fingerprints, or another method. Start from scratch and use only the supplied training data.
  2. Check predictions on different molecules. A separate validation set contains 64,549 molecules. All five error limits must be met; the energy gap is calculated from the two predicted energies.
  3. Compare training times. Run the same method with three fixed random seeds. Each must pass within 60 minutes. The leaderboard uses the median time, including preprocessing, training, evaluation, and output files.

Energy and spin

Excitation energy is the energy needed to reach an excited electronic state, measured in electronvolts (eV). Singlet and triplet denote total electron spin 0 and 1. The model predicts S₁ and T₁; the evaluator derives their difference.

Absorption strength

The dimensionless oscillator strength f describes the strength of the S₀ → S₁ absorption transition. A low excitation energy does not imply strong absorption. The score includes both a logarithmic transform of f and error on stronger transitions.

Training, validation, and the final check

Molecules with the same scaffold—a core pattern of atoms and bonds—or the same connectivity-family key stay in the same split. This reduces the chance of learning on one molecule and being tested on a near duplicate. It does not remove every similar molecule.

After the three runs are fixed, we also report predictions on 31,148 molecules from mostly rare families. This extra check shows how the method transfers to less familiar structures. It does not determine the leaderboard ranking.
